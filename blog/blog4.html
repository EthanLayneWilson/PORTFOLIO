Flames Worth Tending

Small everyday efforts that keep the inner fire alive—and why they’re worth tending.

BY ETHAN WILSON / November 10 2026

ACCOMPANYING TRACK A Pale White Dot Periphery
0:00 / 0:00

רַעְיוֹן ra'ayon - idea, concept, notion, conception, thought

A leaning fence post catches your eye before a straight one does. But if you’re trying to figure out why it’s leaning, you have to look farther down the line. The wire might be pulling harder from one side, or the ground might have softened around the base. You can push the post upright and make it look better for a while. The next time the wire pulls, you’ll find out whether you fixed anything.

I think about that when I consider what I lean on when I’m having a hard time. Some things hold up better than I expect.

A conversation I nearly put off. A few pages read carefully. An evening spent doing something useful without checking whether anyone noticed. These can seem like small things until I go long enough without them. Then their absence starts showing up elsewhere. I become impatient with a problem that deserves patience. I struggle to concentrate and decide the work must be wrong for me. Someone asks a reasonable question and gets an answer shaped by everything except the question.

Usually, by then, I’m looking for something substantial to change. A new plan. A different routine. Some large decision that will explain the feeling and give me a clear place to begin. Sometimes a large change is needed. Other times, I need to look farther down the fence.

What's Worth Leaning On

When I look at the photograph, I notice the mountains first. Then my eye comes back to the fence. It runs through the grass and bends near the creek, and some of the rails look like they’ve been there a long time. I like that the photo leaves all of that in. The mountains are what make me want to stop and take a picture. The fence is what makes it feel like a place I know.

Someone put it there because it needed to do something. Someone has probably walked along it looking for a problem that wasn’t obvious from a distance. Its usefulness depends on details that don’t demand much attention until they fail.

Working around a ranch makes those details difficult to ignore. There are posts, staples, tension, weather, ground, and animals with their own opinions about where they ought to be. A fence that looks dependable from the road might tell a different story when you stand beside it.

I suspect I’m much the same.

There are periods when I look reasonably organized while relying on very little that will actually sustain me. I’m getting things done, answering messages, and keeping track of what comes next. But I’m sleeping poorly, rushing conversations, and giving all my quiet moments to whatever happens to be on a screen.

For a while, that arrangement works well enough to avoid questioning it. Then a difficult day arrives and asks more of it.

I’m trying to pay attention before I reach that point. What leaves me better able to meet the day? Who can I speak honestly with? Which activities help me think clearly afterward? What am I reaching for simply because it is available?

I don’t always like the answers. Some of the things I reach for most readily do very little for me. Some of the things that help require an effort I keep postponing.

Picking up a book is easy once I’m reading. Calling someone is often easier once the conversation has begun. Starting the work becomes manageable when I stop imagining the whole undertaking and deal with the part in front of me. The difficulty is often in the few minutes before that happens, when the easier option is still offering itself.

What Holds Together

You don’t finish a fence and permanently graduate from thinking about fences.

There is something mildly offensive about that. You do a job properly, put your tools away, and would like the world to respect the arrangement. Instead, the weather continues. Wood ages. The ground shifts. Something that held last month needs your attention again.

I can accept this more easily with a fence than with myself.

With myself, I keep expecting a good decision to last longer. I decide to be more patient and feel disappointed when patience requires another decision the following afternoon. I have a useful week of studying and expect the next week to begin with the same concentration. I spend time with someone I care about and unconsciously count it as evidence that the relationship is being looked after.

Then enough time passes, and I wonder why something feels neglected. A fair amount of my disappointment comes from expecting things that need regular care to behave like completed tasks. You can finish an assignment. You can submit an application. You can repair a particular break in a fence. Understanding and friendship keep asking something of you after the satisfying part is over. Often, what they ask is ordinary.

Software engineering gives me plenty of practice with this. A concept can seem entirely manageable while someone else explains it. I follow the example, recognize the terms, and feel the pleasant relief of understanding. Then I close the explanation and try to produce something myself. Apparently, recognition and understanding have been sharing a name without consulting me. I return to the material. I work through a smaller example. Eventually, I find the point where I stopped following and started agreeing. Sometimes that takes most of the session.

From the outside, very little has happened. There is no finished project to show. I may have fewer lines of code than when I started. But I’ve found a gap that would have followed me into the next problem. That is useful work, even when it doesn’t feel especially rewarding. The same is true of the small efforts that hold relationships together. Remembering something someone told me and asking about it later. Doing the thing I said I would do after the moment in which I said it has passed. Listening long enough to hear an answer I wasn’t expecting.

There is often no audience for this. Sometimes there isn’t much acknowledgment. A message gets a brief reply. A favor disappears into someone else’s busy day. You show up, help, and go home. I’m trying not to use the size of the response as the measure of the effort. I know how much an ordinary kindness can matter when I’m receiving it. Someone makes room for me in a conversation. Someone notices I’ve been quiet. Someone takes a question seriously when I was worried it would sound foolish. I may give them very little indication of the relief that brought. It would be strange to believe these things matter when they reach me, then require proof every time I offer them. What holds together often does so because someone continues to care when caring has become routine. The work becomes familiar enough to overlook, but its effects accumulate. You learn whom you can trust. You become a little more capable. A conversation becomes easier because it isn’t the first honest one you’ve had.

These things take time to become dependable. I want to give them enough time.

What Gives Way

“What is ‘familiarly known’ is not properly known, just for the reason that it is ‘familiar’.” 1

I enjoy the first few days of a project. I can picture what it might become, and almost any work I do feels like progress. A few weeks later, the work can be as ordinary as fixing a layout on a small screen or reading a paragraph aloud because one sentence keeps tripping me up. Some nights I spend more time deciding what to take out than I spent putting it there.

I’ve come to like some of that work. I like reopening something I made months ago and finding a better way to do one part of it. I like reading an old note and realizing I can answer the question I wrote down. The excitement of starting has usually faded by then, but I get a different kind of satisfaction from knowing a piece of work well enough to see what it needs.

When I think about tending a fire, I think about the moment when there’s still enough heat to catch a small piece of dry wood. You can sit there awhile, let it catch, and see if it needs anything else. I want to leave more time for that kind of attention in the things I care about. It’s hard to give them much of it when I’m already thinking about the next thing I could start.

Some lessons come from studying a concept. Others come from trying an approach, finding its limits, and revising it. I need both. Studying gives me a foundation, while building something helps me discover which parts I understand and which parts still need attention.

That has meant making room to be a beginner. Coming from work where I had familiar tools and practical experience, it was an adjustment to need help with things that might seem basic to an experienced developer. I had to become comfortable asking questions, revisiting material, and spending longer on a problem than I initially expected.

My experience with cars and equipment gives me a useful perspective. You do not become familiar with a system simply by looking at it once. You learn through working with it, understanding how its parts connect, and encountering problems that make you look more closely. I am finding that programming requires the same willingness to build understanding over time.

With a gasoline engine, all you need to run is fuel, air, and spark. They need to arrive in the right amounts and at the right time, and compression matters too. If the engine will not run, I can work through those requirements and narrow down what is missing. Experience gives me a sequence of checks, even when I do not yet know the cause. Code requires a different set of checks. A program might fail because of an unexpected input, a condition that never becomes true, or a function that changes something another part of the program depends on. There is no single checklist that explains every failure, believe me I checked. So I have to understand what the program expects, follow what it actually does, and find where those two things diverge. Now, my mechanical background does help with the patience and investigation, but I still have to learn the rules of the system in front of me. Around engines, there is an old saying: “There’s no replacement for displacement.” In programming, however, adding more code rarely gives me the same promise. Sometimes the best improvement actually comes from removing a few lines, choosing a better algorithm, or avoiding work that has already been done. Memoization fits that last idea: keeping a useful result available instead of repeating the calculation. This archive serves a similar purpose in my learning, preserving the reasoning behind a solution so I have something to build on the next time I encounter a familiar problem.

When something breaks, I try to understand why before moving on. Getting the program working again matters, but I also want to know what caused the problem. Was I misunderstanding how a function behaved? Was I making an assumption about the input? Had I changed something without considering where else it was used? Answering those questions makes the fix useful beyond that one situation. There is a temptation to move quickly once an error disappears, especially after spending a long time trying to solve it. But pausing to explain the solution helps me check my understanding. If I cannot describe why the change worked, there is probably still something worth investigating. Even a brief note about the cause can give me something to return to later.

I am also learning to recognize when a problem needs to become smaller. If an entire program feels overwhelming, I can focus on one function or test one piece of logic. If a layout is not working, I can examine one section before adjusting the whole page. A smaller problem is easier to navigate through, and what I learn from it often helps me understand better the larger one.

Progress often comes from those small steps: understanding a function, simplifying a layout, or choosing a data structure that better fits the task. Sometimes it means recognizing an error sooner than I would have before, or being able to explain code that once confused me. Those changes certainly are easy to overlook, but they show that the time spent learning is making a difference.

“Goals are good for setting a direction, but systems are best for making progress.” 2

Making room to learn means allowing enough time for that process. I want to keep building projects, while also giving myself the chance to understand the decisions behind them. Each problem I work through adds to the experience I can bring to the next one.

What's Worth Tending

A fire makes some of its needs fairly visible. If the wood is wet, wishing for more heat won’t dry it immediately. If everything is packed too tightly, adding another piece may make matters worse. Leave it alone long enough, and the bright part disappears before the last heat does. I’m less observant with the things I care about. I can lose enthusiasm and assume I’ve lost interest. I can become tired of the effort and forget what the effort was doing for me. When something no longer feels new, I’m tempted to interpret the change as evidence that I should move on.

Sometimes I should. But an uneventful afternoon is a poor judge of an entire pursuit. There are things I continue to care about through periods when caring feels like work. Studying something difficult. Making time for people. Keeping promises. Paying attention to what I believe and how closely my choices reflect it. I don’t need every attempt to produce a feeling of purpose. I need to look honestly at what repeated attention is making possible. An album listened to from start to finish can give an evening a shape. I notice a part I’d forgotten, hear something differently, or sit still long enough to stop carrying the day around in my shoulders. A few pages of a book can take a thought farther than I would have taken it on my own. A conversation can remind me that the problem occupying my whole attention is one part of a much larger life. These are modest things. That is partly why I trust them. I can return to them without requiring an entirely different set of circumstances. When I have less to give, the task needs to be honest about its size.

“Understand this whole subject” is difficult to act on after a long day. “Work through this example and identify what confuses me” gives me somewhere to begin. “Be a better friend” is something I can approve of indefinitely. “Call him back” requires me to pick up the phone. Small actions won’t repair every problem. Some circumstances are painful, complicated, and slow to change. I don’t want to give a tidy routine more credit than it deserves. But a manageable action can give me somewhere to place my next effort. When everything feels unfinished, that matters. So does being willing to return after neglecting something. A missed day can remain a missed day. I don’t have to turn it into an account of why I always fail, or make the return so ambitious that I postpone that too.

Open the book where I stopped. Begin with what I remember. Make the call without spending another week feeling guilty about it. Accept that some ground has been lost and give it time.

I’m increasingly grateful for things that allow this kind of return. A relationship in which an honest conversation is still possible. A subject that continues to interest me after a frustrating week. Music that sounds familiar without becoming empty. Work that lets me be useful.

Over time, I can see which efforts leave something good behind them. I can see where repeated attention has made a person, a skill, or a commitment more dependable. That helps me decide what deserves the time I have. I still like the satisfaction of a finished job. I like a problem solved, a fence standing straight, and an evening in which I can point to something and say I got that done. I’m learning to make room for the jobs that won’t stay finished. The call that needs making again. The lesson that needs revisiting. The attention that has to be offered to the person in front of me, even if I was attentive yesterday. These are things I want to keep returning to. They have held up under enough ordinary days that I trust them.

When I have less to give, I want to remember where to give it.
